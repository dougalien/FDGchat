Chapter 2: Observational Registers: Galaxies, Gravity, Voids, and Boundaries

Observational Registers: Galaxies, Gravity, Voids, and Boundaries

Abstract

The finite deformable redshift framework is extended across additional observational registers. A previous BAO-CMB shared-ruler failure identified a sharp recovery transition. Recasting that result as a dimensionless projection-depth scale gives chi, defined from the CMB-to-BAO separate-scale ratio 0.390906. This coordinate places the BAO regime deep in the transparent interior, JWST z approximately 8 galaxies on the approach to the transition, the reconstructed projection break near chi approximately 0.20, and the CMB-side scale near chi approximately 0.39. These chi values are normalized depth coordinates and should not be confused with A = ln(1+z) or with redshift itself. A Labbé-Baggen JWST mass-size audit shows that a finite-geometry projection shoulder moves published z > 7 galaxy properties toward lower inferred stellar mass, larger effective radius, and lower compactness. An expanded JWST-PRIMAL spectroscopic test does not isolate a clean residual color or sky-position signal after line strength, ionization, Lyα damping behavior, and UV slope are considered. The gravity extension adds the SPARC radial acceleration relation, selected dissociative cluster offsets, and large-scale basins of attraction. The cleanest gravity result is the galaxy-scale acceleration transition: SPARC binned data recover a characteristic acceleration near 10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻², with the observed-to-baryonic response rising systematically below that scale. An SDSS DR7 VAST morphology screen asks whether matter-void foam architecture is better organized by the full finite-response coordinate D = A G than by redshift or A = ln(1+z) alone. D improves several independent shape metrics, while standard redshift remains competitive and the SDSS depth is too shallow to reach the proposed transition range. The field-equation bridge retains general relativity as the local high-acceleration limit while adding a finite-geometry correction term that activates near regime thresholds. The black-hole extension treats u = 2GM/(Rc²) as a compactness-regime variable that approaches finite saturation rather than physical infinity.

Keywords: JWST, high-redshift galaxies, finite geometry, redshift, BAO-CMB tension, projection effects, PRIMAL, DAWN JWST Archive, SPARC, radial acceleration relation, galaxy clusters, basins of attraction

The organizing idea is that the universe may not have one common measurement scale across all registers. Redshift, luminosity distance, angular-size distance, weak-field gravity, void architecture, and compactness may remain locally measurable while still requiring different finite-response coordinates when compared across regimes.

1. Introduction

A redshift path-response analysis previously tested whether redshift can be treated as accumulated response inside finite deformable geometry, rather than as a purely expansion-based distance clock. Its strongest result was a set of constraints: the supernova redshift-distance relation could be represented by a compact declining-response law, but the same mapping failed sharply when BAO and CMB information were forced to share one acoustic-ruler calibration. The recovery target preserved the low- and intermediate-redshift BAO range while changing high-redshift projection rapidly.

High-redshift JWST galaxies provide an independent place to test the same conversion issue. The relevant distinction is between direct observables and cosmology-dependent derived quantities. Spectroscopic redshift, observed flux, sky position, line ratios, equivalent widths, and continuum slopes are close to measurement. Stellar mass, physical radius, luminosity, star-formation rate, age, and number density require a cosmological conversion from redshift to distance and time. If that conversion is incomplete at high redshift, some galaxies can appear too massive, too compact, or too old even when the measured spectra are correct.

The conversion audit asks whether derived masses, sizes, colors, and sky/redshift structures behave in a way compatible with a projection-register shift. Mass-size quantities move in the direction expected from a finite-geometry projection correction. Spectral color and sky-position structure do not show a clean residual geometric signal in the present PRIMAL tests.

2. Dimensionless finite-geometry depth coordinate

The BAO-CMB analysis identified an effective separate-scale ratio between CMB-side and BAO-side acoustic calibration. S_BAO is the effective BAO-side calibration scale and S_CMB is the effective CMB-side calibration scale. Their ratio is used here as the organizing depth scale. The visible-to-total finite-geometry radius fraction is written as

\[f_{\mathrm{vis}} = S_{\mathrm{CMB}} / S_{\mathrm{BAO}} = 0.390906 \tag{2-1}\]

See the Quantitative Appendix for the separate-scale ratio and calibration details.

This ratio gives a total-to-visible radius ratio of 2.558 and a visible-to-total volume fraction of 0.0597. It also gives an attenuation-like number, -ln(f(vis)) = 0.939, close to one optical-depth-style unit. Its main use here is as a dimensionless depth coordinate:

\[\chi(z) = f_{\mathrm{vis}} \ln(1 + z) / \ln\left[1 + z(CMB)\right] \tag{2-2}\]

Equation (2) leaves redshift as the measured quantity but maps it onto a compact depth scale. With z(CMB) = 1089.92, the CMB-side scale lies at chi = 0.390906 by construction. Lower redshift observables then occupy smaller positions inside this finite-geometry depth.

Coordinate discipline is essential. The redshift-response analysis used A = ln(1+z) as the accumulated path response and G(d) as the local response rate. The chi coordinate is only a finite-depth display coordinate scaled by the CMB-to-BAO separate-scale ratio. These quantities are related, but they are not interchangeable. The normalized transition coordinate X = A/A_t, where A_t = ln(1+z_t) and z_t = 34.5754, gives a common comparison coordinate. In this notation, X = 1 marks the reconstructed recovery transition, analogous to a one-focal-length transition scale.

3. Data and methods

3.1 Projection transition displayed as T(chi)

The same transition behavior is displayed in chi-space as a compact boundary-layer recovery function:

\[T(\chi) = 1 - \Delta \chi^m / (\chi^m + \chi_t^m) \tag{2-3}\]

In redshift space, the best recovery case used Δ = 0.627714, zₜ = 34.5754, and m = 4. In the chi display, that midpoint becomes chiₜ = 0.1996. This display is useful because the transition is described as occurring at about one-fifth of the assumed finite-geometry depth, rather than being tied only to a large redshift value. It is the same transition behavior shown in a different coordinate, not a new independent derivation.

See the Quantitative Appendix for transition-function definitions and stage-output mapping.

3.2 JWST mass-size projection audit

The first galaxy audit uses the Labbé et al. red massive candidate sample and the Baggen et al. structural subset. Labbé et al. reported red candidate massive galaxies at z approximately 7.4-9.1, and Baggen et al. measured compact effective radii for the subset with usable structural fits. The present calculation does not refit SEDs or morphologies. It asks how published mass and radius values move under a simple projection conversion.

A fourth-power projection shoulder centered in the JWST redshift range is used as a stress test:

\[\Phi(z) = 1 - \Delta z^4 / (z^4 + z_s^4) \tag{2-4}\]
Technical note

The amplitude Δ = 0.627714 is retained from the recovery transition, while the midpoint is shifted to zₛ = 8. This deliberately tests a weaker high-redshift shoulder in the red massive galaxy regime rather than the main BAO-CMB transition.

3.4 Gravity-regime audit

The gravity audit adds three real-data diagnostics. The galaxy-scale test uses the published SPARC binned radial acceleration relation, which compares the observed centripetal acceleration from rotation curves with the acceleration predicted from the observed baryonic mass distribution [50] [51]. The binned relation was fit with the empirical RAR form:

\[g_{\mathrm{obs}} = \frac{g_{\mathrm{bar}}}{1 - \exp\left[-\left(g_{\mathrm{bar}}/g^\dagger\right)^{1/2}\right]} \tag{2-8}\]

where g† is the characteristic acceleration scale. The response ratio gobs/gbar is then used as a dimensionless measure of gravitational-regime departure. The cluster-scale test uses a literature-coded set of dissociative mergers, where gravitational-lensing centers, hot X-ray gas, and galaxy concentrations are spatially separated. The large-scale test uses published velocity-flow basin results from Laniakea and Cosmicflows-4 to ask whether gravitational flow domains provide a larger-scale analogue of regime boundaries [52] [53].

4. Results

4.1 The chi coordinate gives a compact ordering of regimes

The depth coordinate organizes the main regimes in a simple order. The BAO high end lies at chi = 0.0667. The JWST z approximately 8 regime lies near chi = 0.1228, and a z = 14.32 object would lie near chi = 0.1525. The reconstructed transition midpoint lies at chi = 0.1996, while the CMB-side scale lies at chi = 0.390906. In this ordering, JWST z > 7 galaxies do not lie at the main transition, but they do sit on the approach toward it.

4.2 Mass-size quantities move in the expected direction

The Labbé-Baggen audit gives the most constructive result. The median log stellar mass shifts from 9.7369 to 9.4260. The median effective radius increases from 125.5 pc to 190.0 pc. The median compactness proxy log10(M\*/Rₑ²) decreases from 5.4826 to 4.9411. These changes show that the correction acts in the direction needed to reduce the apparent too-massive and too-compact tension.

4.3 Spectral color does not isolate a geometric residual

The larger PRIMAL test is more restrictive. Higher βUV corresponds to a redder UV continuum. In the full PRIMAL sample, βUV becomes bluer, not redder, with increasing redshift. The descriptive Pearson correlation between βUV and redshift is -0.226. Correlations with spectral features also show that conventional galaxy physics is important: βUV correlates with D(Lyα), \[OIII\]+Hβ equivalent width, and O32. A regression against MUV, log EW(\[OIII\]+Hβ), D(Lyα), and log O32 explains a modest fraction of the βUV variance, with R² = 0.124. The residual βUV trend against chi remains weak.

4.4 Multi-field sky-position tests do not show a simple boundary pattern

The sky-position tests are also limiting. In the PRIMAL sample, redshift shows little linear dependence on RA or Dec, with r = -0.061 for z versus RA and r = -0.074 for z versus Dec. The residual βUV field is also weakly correlated with sky position. These results do not rule out a geometric interpretation, because the public sample is assembled from many JWST programs with different selection functions. They do mean that the present data do not show a simple RA/Dec phase-boundary pattern.

4.5 Gravity-regime tests

The gravity tests are included as a bridge from projection-depth behavior to local and large-scale gravitational response. They ask whether gravitational observables contain natural transition scales or domain boundaries that can be compared with the finite-geometry language developed above.

The galaxy acceleration test gives the strongest result. The SPARC binned radial acceleration relation is fit by Equation (2-8) with g† = 9.89 × 10⁻¹¹ m s⁻². The observed-to-baryonic response reaches about a factor of two at gbar = 6.07 × 10⁻¹¹ m s⁻². Above the characteristic scale, the relation approaches the one-to-one baryonic prediction. Below it, the observed gravitational response increasingly exceeds the baryonic prediction (Figure 7). This is a clear gravitational-regime transition in a dimensionless response ratio.

The dissociative-cluster test is informative but less clean. The Bullet Cluster and MACS J0025.4-1222 show lensing/gravity peaks separated from the hot X-ray gas and aligned more closely with the collisionless component [54] [55]. Abell 520 is a counterexample-like system with a dark-core morphology associated with the X-ray gas and poor in bright galaxies [56] [57]. Abell 56 and related bullet-like mergers show additional offsets but complex merger geometry [58]. In a 38-cluster lensing/X-ray sample, 45 percent of clusters had projected offsets larger than 10 arcsec [59]. These results show that hot baryonic gas, galaxies, and gravitational lensing centers can separate, but they do not by themselves distinguish finite-geometry response from collisionless dark matter (Figure 8).

The attractor test is best treated as a conceptual large-scale analogue. Laniakea was defined through velocity-flow watersheds, with galaxy motions organized into an inward-flowing basin after subtracting mean expansion and longer-range flows [52]. Cosmicflows-4 work extends this idea probabilistically and gives a slight preference for Laniakea being part of the larger Shapley basin; the Sloan Great Wall basin is the largest recovered basin in that analysis [53]. These results support the language of gravitational domains and watershed boundaries. They are not independent proof of finite geometry because peculiar-velocity reconstructions are model-dependent (Figure 9).

4.6 Matter-void foam morphology screen and finite-response screening

The projection-depth, spectroscopic-residual, and gravitational-response tests are extended to matter-void morphology. The screen tests whether the large-scale distribution of void space and web-bounded foam structure is organized by the full finite-response coordinate, rather than by redshift ordering alone.

The test uses the public SDSS DR7 VAST void catalogs, which provide complementary void populations found with VoidFinder and with V2 watershed methods using VIDE and REVOLVER pruning [60]. The Planck 2018 VAST release reports 1163 VoidFinder voids, 531 V2/VIDE voids, and 518 V2/REVOLVER voids in the SDSS DR7 main-survey volume to z = 0.114, with effective radii and centers included in ASCII catalog files [61] [62]. This is shallower than the desired transition test, but it is useful because the same volume-limited galaxy sample can be inspected with large-void and watershed-style definitions.

Technical note

Because the catalog reaches only z = 0.114, it probes the early side of the response curve. It is an early-range morphology screen for D = A G, not a direct sample of the proposed deeper transition region.

VoidFinder and watershed methods are treated as complementary shape filters. VoidFinder is used as a proxy for large coherent inter-web void regions. VIDE and REVOLVER are used as proxies for nested basin-like void structure within the foam. Watershed algorithms segment the density field into basins and can preserve hierarchical structure, while VIDE uses a Voronoi tessellation, a ZOBOV-based watershed transform, and a hierarchy tree to construct and analyze void catalogs [63] [64] [65]. Differences among void definitions become measurable morphology signals.

The earlier coordinate-only morphology screen compared standard redshift z with the accumulated finite-geometry coordinate

\[A(z) = \ln(1 + z) \tag{2-10}\]

That screen was incomplete because it did not include the response-weakening term used in the finite deformable-geometry model. The fuller screen therefore compares void metrics against four predictors:

\[z, A(z) = \ln(1 + z), G(d) = g_0 / \left[1 + d/d_b\right], D(z) = A(z)G(d). \tag{2-11}\]

When D is used later as a response-function argument, it is normalized as D_n = D/D0, where D0 is a chosen reference response scale with the same units as D. The raw D is retained for the morphology screen.

See the Quantitative Appendix for D_n normalization and response-function bookkeeping.

Technical note

For comparison with the projection transition, the same redshift bins can also be expressed as X = A/A_t. This X coordinate reports where a sample lies relative to the recovery transition and complements the z, A, G, and D predictor comparison.

For this first catalog-level test, d is treated as a normalized redshift-distance proxy, d_norm = z / z_max, and g0 is set to 1. The response scale db is scanned over db = 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50, and 1.00. This normalization makes the screen dimensionless and reproducible; full cosmological distance reconstruction is a separate step.

A foam index is defined as the ratio of watershed-defined voids to large VoidFinder voids within the same redshift or response-coordinate bin:

\[I_{\mathrm{foam}}(z) = N_{\mathrm{watershed}}(z) / N_{\mathrm{VoidFinder}}(z). \tag{2-13}\]

The tested morphology vector is therefore

\[S(z) = \left[I_{\mathrm{foam}}, R_{\mathrm{eff}}, Ve, C_{\mathrm{shape}}\right], \tag{2-14}\]

where Ifoam is the foam-index proxy, Reff is effective radius, Ve is void ellipticity where available, and C_shape is a hierarchy/connectivity proxy where catalog columns allow it. Dimensional vector entries are standardized before comparison. Each metric is compared against z, A, G, and D using linear, quadratic, and two-segment piecewise fits, with neutral labels assigned from R2, RMSE, monotonicity, and Spearman trend diagnostics.

The most important outcome is the pattern of where the winning labels occur. D = A G performs better than A alone for several independent morphology metrics, including the REVOLVER foam index, mean ellipticity in both REVOLVER and VIDE, and VoidFinder median radius. This indicates that adding the response-weakening term G can organize some shape quantities that are not clarified by accumulated deformation A alone. The best response-scale value in the tested grid is db = 1.0, which means the preferred response weakening is gradual across the SDSS range. A sharply decaying response function is not favored by this early-range morphology screen.

Standard redshift remains the best predictor for several metrics, including the mean foam index and selected radius and ellipticity summaries. The full finite-response variable is therefore worth testing in a deeper object-level survey [66], while the shallow SDSS screen remains subordinate to the stronger JWST mass-size and SPARC constraints.

The possible pressure behavior is not a negative-pressure claim. The relevant transition is the recovery transition recast as chi approximately 0.20, or equivalently X = 1. That transition corresponds to z_t = 34.5754 and A_t = ln(1+z_t) approximately 3.572, not A approximately 0.20. The SDSS screen samples only X approximately 0.03, so it is an early-range morphology screen for the D = A G variable rather than a direct pressure-transition test. A decisive shape test would require a much deeper structure probe or another observable that samples the X approximately 1 regime.

5. Discussion

Three classes of observational registers are compared. The JWST sections audit projection conversion using derived galaxy properties while holding direct observables such as redshift, flux, and spectra as close to measurement as possible. The gravity sections recast acceleration and compactness behavior as finite-response regimes. The foam-shape and black-hole sections define shape and boundary targets. The interpretive weight is unequal: JWST mass-size conversion and the SPARC transition are current constraints, while matter-void and compactness results are early-stage constraints and model targets.

5.1 What strengthens the model

The strongest positive result is the mass-size direction of effect. Under the projection shoulder, published high-redshift galaxies become less massive, larger, and less compact. This is exactly the direction required if part of the early-galaxy tension comes from the expansion-based conversion between redshift, luminosity distance, angular-size distance, and physical age. The precise amplitude of the chosen shoulder matters less here than measurability: raw redshift, flux, angular size, and spectra can be held fixed while derived masses and sizes are recomputed under an alternative projection law.

Because the PRIMAL color and sky-position audits do not provide independent support, the strongest current JWST leverage remains in the mass-size conversion test rather than in spectral residuals.

5.2 Spectral and void-shape constraints

The PRIMAL results constrain the projection interpretation. UV color and sky position do not provide independent support for a finite-geometry boundary. Strong rest-optical emission lines, ionization state, Lyα damping behavior, dust, selection, and field structure all influence the appearance of high-redshift galaxies. The expanded sample trends bluer with redshift, and the residual color signal after spectral-feature correction remains weak. A geometric interpretation is therefore better tested first through mass-size-age conversion rather than red color alone.

The void-shape screen is also constrained by depth. Its value is methodological: it shows how to formulate a deeper matter-void shape test against z, A, G, and D. It does not change the stronger constraints set by JWST mass-size conversion and the SPARC transition.

5.3 Required projection-conversion test

A stronger projection-conversion test requires spectroscopic redshifts and line diagnostics from PRIMAL or the DAWN JWST Archive, homogeneous morphology and size measurements, and a controlled projection conversion applied to luminosity distance and angular-size distance separately. The decisive question is whether finite-geometry conversion systematically reduces the mass-size-age tension without degrading direct spectroscopic observables. If the effect survives across multiple fields and independent reduction pipelines, it becomes a stronger test of projection-regime physics. If it disappears after improved SED modeling, AGN screening, dust correction, and morphology homogenization, then the JWST anomaly is better treated as ordinary high-redshift galaxy astrophysics.

5.4 Gravity-regime implication

The gravity audit strengthens one part of the finite-geometry program and limits another. The strongest addition is the SPARC acceleration transition. It shows a repeatable, dimensionless departure in gravitational response near 10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻², independent of the redshift-depth coordinate. This is the natural gravity analogue of a regime boundary: baryons remain the local source term, while the effective gravitational response changes below a characteristic acceleration scale.

The cluster and attractor tests are useful but less decisive. Dissociative clusters show that lensing response, hot gas, and galaxies can occupy different observational registers during mergers, but collisionless dark matter already explains much of this behavior in the standard framework. Velocity-flow basins such as Laniakea and the Shapley basin provide a suggestive large-scale vocabulary of gravitational domains, yet those reconstructions depend on expansion-subtracted peculiar velocities. The result is a hierarchy of test strength: galaxy acceleration gives a clean regime transition; cluster mergers provide ambiguous decoupling cases; attractor basins provide a large-scale conceptual analogue.

5.5 Finite-geometry correction term for the GR limit

The gravity result suggests a conservative route for connecting the model to relativistic gravity. The local success of general relativity should be retained. The finite-deformable framework can be written as an effective correction that becomes negligible in high-acceleration, weak-boundary regimes and activates only when a system crosses a deformation threshold. A compact form is:

\[G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} + F_{\mu\nu} = (8\pi G/c^4)T_{\mu\nu} \tag{2-15}\]

Here F with tensor indices is not introduced as a new matter component. It represents an effective finite-geometry response term. The term must vanish where ordinary tests of GR are already successful and must be constrained by conservation requirements. A useful phenomenological decomposition is:

\[F_{\mu\nu} = \alpha B_{\mu\nu} C(\chi, \eta, u, D_n) \tag{2-16}\]

In this notation, B with tensor indices sets the direction and structure of the correction, α sets its amplitude, and C is a dimensionless compliance function. The regime variables used here are:

\[\eta = g_{\mathrm{bar}}/g^\dagger, u = 2GM/(Rc^2), D_n = D/D_0 \tag{2-17}\]

The variables describe different kinds of boundary approach: η is the galaxy acceleration regime, u is the Schwarzschild compactness measure, and D_n is the normalized morphology-response coordinate. The symbol ψ is reserved for the Chapter 4 conformal factor. The SPARC result gives the first empirical calibration. The binned relation is well described by a dimensionless compliance factor:

\[C_g(\eta) = 1 - \exp(-\sqrt{\eta}), g_{\mathrm{obs}}/g_{\mathrm{bar}} = 1/C_g(\eta) \tag{2-18}\]

Equation (2-18) recasts the radial acceleration relation as a weak-field finite-geometry compliance law. At η much greater than 1, the response ratio approaches unity and ordinary baryonic gravity is recovered. At η below order unity, the geometry behaves as if it becomes more compliant, and the observed response exceeds the baryonic prediction. This interpretation has an empirical relation to MOND and relativistic MOND-like theories, while the present model treats the transition as a finite-geometry regime effect rather than as a completed modified-gravity theory [67] [68] [69].

The weak-field comparison also sits beside MOND and relativistic-MOND review literature. The present model instead uses SPARC/RAR behavior as a finite-geometry compliance register, not as a direct adoption of MOND dynamics [81].

See the Quantitative Appendix for response-function notation used by the register bridge.

5.6 Black holes as compactness-boundary tests

The black-hole compactness register supplies a boundary-condition target. It sets what a finite-deformable model must satisfy at the compactness limit while remaining consistent with the chi, JWST, SPARC, and void-shape results.

The black-hole case is the next natural boundary test because it pushes the same question to the strongest compactness limit. The dimensionless variable u = 2GM/(Rc²) compares a system's Schwarzschild radius with the radius at which the mass is being evaluated. For a nonrotating exterior solution, u = 1 marks the horizon scale. The classical singularity is a further extrapolation toward R -> 0. In the finite-deformable framework, the horizon is not treated as a physical infinity. It is treated as the entry into a compactness regime where the relationships among mass, time, distance, and curvature may no longer be described by extending ordinary exterior coordinates without limit.

A finite-saturation target can be written as:

\[S(u) = u^m / (u^m + u_c^m), u_c \approx 1 \tag{2-19}\]

Here S(u) is a compactness-response function, uₕ is the order-unity threshold, and m controls transition sharpness. The function is small in weak compactness, rises rapidly near the horizon-scale threshold when m is large, and approaches a finite value instead of diverging. It defines a boundary condition: preserve the exterior behavior that makes GR successful while replacing the singular endpoint with a finite interior response. This framing is consistent in spirit with regular or nonsingular black-hole programs, which seek finite interior geometries rather than curvature singularities, but the present motivation is finite-geometry regime saturation [1] [2] [70].

The schematic black-hole contribution to the finite-geometry term can then be written as:

\[F_{\mu\nu}(BH) = \alpha_{BH} B_{\mu\nu}(BH) S(u) \tag{2-20}\]

In this expression, αBH sets the amplitude of the compactness-regime correction, Bμν(BH) sets its tensor structure, and S(u) controls when it activates. A future theory would have to derive this term from an action or another conservation-compatible principle. It would also have to recover the exterior Schwarzschild/Kerr behavior tested by black-hole shadows and gravitational-wave mergers while changing only the interior or near-boundary extrapolation that would otherwise produce a singularity [71] [72].

A future extension would also need to show that any S(u)-driven interior modification leaves exterior lensing, ringdown, and shadow observables unchanged to current precision, while remaining derivable from an action-compatible or otherwise conservation-compatible Fμν term.

5.7 Shape-test implication and pressure-transition target

The matter-void morphology screen adds a shape register to the projection and gravity registers. High-redshift galaxy sections test whether derived galaxy properties are altered by projection-depth conversion. Gravity sections test whether acceleration and compactness regimes can be written as finite-response boundaries. The void test asks whether underdense space and web-bounded foam structure organize under the finite deformable response variable.

The SDSS VAST catalogs show that the question is measurable, and the full response coordinate D = A G improves several independent shape metrics relative to A alone. Standard z remains the strongest predictor for other metrics, and the available SDSS redshift range samples only the very early part of the normalized transition coordinate.

The pressure question belongs after the shape test. In the present framework, the pressure-transition target belongs to the chi approximately 0.20 / X = 1 recovery regime. A physical test would ask whether matter-coupled deformation response weakens toward a vacuum-like state as X approaches 1. The pressure-like quantity would decrease from positive response toward near-zero vacuum response, not cross into a required negative-pressure regime. The current SDSS test cannot reach that regime; it only tests whether low-redshift matter-void foam metrics behave coherently under z, A, G, and D.

6. Limitations

The present analysis uses processed catalog products and descriptive correlations. The Labbé-Baggen mass-size sample is small. The PRIMAL sample is larger but not a uniform all-sky survey, and field selection remains important. The residual-color regression uses a compact model and does not include full hierarchical treatment. Full SED refitting, morphology remeasurement, covariance propagation, and AGN removal are outside this pass. The chi coordinate depends on f(vis) = 0.390906 as the organizing scale. The SPARC result is based on binned acceleration data, the cluster comparison uses a literature-coded merger set, and the attractor analysis depends on peculiar-velocity reconstructions built on a background cosmological model. The finite-geometry correction term is phenomenological, and the black-hole saturation function is specified as a boundary target. A full relativistic theory would need to define F with tensor indices from an action or other dynamical principle and show compatibility with the Bianchi identity, energy-momentum conservation, gravitational lensing, Solar System tests, gravitational waves, black-hole observations, and exterior Schwarzschild/Kerr behavior. The SDSS DR7 VAST foam screen uses a normalized distance proxy d_norm = z/z_max and reaches only A = ln(1+z) approximately 0.108, which is X approximately 0.030 relative to the recovery transition; it therefore does not sample the chi approximately 0.20 / X = 1 pressure-to-vacuum regime directly.

7. Conclusions

Empirical findings

• Interpreting the separate-scale ratio 0.390906 as a finite-geometry depth scale gives a useful unitless coordinate, chi.

• In chi, BAO lies near 0.067, JWST z approximately 8 galaxies near 0.123, the reconstructed projection transition near 0.200, and the CMB-side scale at 0.391.

• The JWST mass-size audit moves derived galaxy properties in the expected direction: lower stellar mass, larger radius, and lower compactness.

• Expanded PRIMAL spectroscopy does not isolate a clean residual color or sky-position signal after spectral-feature effects are considered. The strongest current JWST leverage remains in mass-size conversion.

• The gravity extension identifies the SPARC radial acceleration relation as the cleanest gravitational-regime transition, with g† near 10⁻¹⁰ m s⁻² and a 2× response near 6.07 × 10⁻¹¹ m s⁻².

Technical note

• The SPARC acceleration transition can be written as a weak-field compliance law, gobs/gbar = 1/Cg(η), where η = gbar/g† and Cg(η) = 1 - exp(-√η).

• The SDSS VAST matter-void screen treats the cosmic web and its void spaces as a foam-like architecture, using VoidFinder as a proxy for large coherent inter-web voids and V2/VIDE and V2/REVOLVER as proxies for watershed-defined basin structure.

• The full finite-response coordinate D = A G performs better than A alone for several independent shape metrics, including REVOLVER foam index, VIDE and REVOLVER mean ellipticity, and VoidFinder median radius. The result remains mixed because standard z is better for several other metrics.

Conceptual targets

• Dissociative clusters and large-scale basins show baryon/lensing and velocity-flow decoupling, but those tests remain dynamically complex and model-dependent.

• A conservative field-equation bridge is possible: retain GR as the local high-acceleration limit and add an effective finite-geometry term Fμν that activates only near regime thresholds.

• The SDSS VAST screen reaches only A approximately 0.108, which is X approximately 0.030 relative to the recovery transition. The chi approximately 0.20 / X = 1 pressure-to-vacuum target therefore requires a deeper or different object-level structure probe, evaluated first as a shape-transition test.

• Gravity testing requires a full SPARC galaxy-by-galaxy finite-compliance fit, followed by a homogeneous cluster-offset catalog and controlled attractor-basin analysis.

Technical note

• Black holes are treated as a compactness-boundary target. The model should treat u = 2GM/(Rc²) as a compactness-regime variable and seek finite saturation S(u), rather than allowing a mathematical singularity to become a physical endpoint.

• The black-hole extension requires exterior GR behavior, compatibility with black-hole shadows and gravitational-wave mergers, and finite behavior as compactness crosses the horizon-scale regime.
